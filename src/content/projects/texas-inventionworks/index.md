Since August 2024, I've been a student technician at <a href="https://inventionworks.engr.utexas.edu/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><span class="org-mark is-tiw" aria-hidden="true"></span>Texas Inventionworks (TIW)</a>, UT Austin's makerspace. Our role is to maintain the space as we work shifts in the different areas of TIW, such as the Digital Fabrication Space, the Laser Cutting Space, and the main desk in the Garage. Our spaces extend even further, with a full electronics space where we can solder and a machining area for manual machines and CNC machines. In addition to the everyday shifts, we each join a team. I started on the Machine Shop Team, teaching students how to use the manual mill, manual lathe, and CNC. I then moved to the Woodshop where I soon became lead and taught students how to use the table, miter, and band saws, in addition to planers, routers, and even a panel saw. This year, I'm on the Design Team, where I work on workshops and projects to better the space.

<h2 class="text-centered">Trainings</h2>

<p class="text-full">Trainings are one of the reasons I first wanted to join the space. They range from everyday trainings on FDM printers like the Bambu P1P, given to students every even hour as part of our Headquarters shift, to specialized trainings students book for machinery from manual mills to router tables. Below are some of the specialized trainings I gave.</p>

<h3 class="text-centered">Machine Shop Trainings</h3>

![Turning aluminum on the manual lathe](./images/lathe-1.jpg "right-pair")
![A machined aluminum ring from the lathe training](./images/training-ring.jpg)

<p class="caption caption-sm">Turning aluminum on the lathe</p>

<p class="caption caption-sm">A ring from the lathe training</p>

As part of the Machine Shop Team, I ran multiple two-hour training sessions on the manual mill and the manual lathe every week. The sessions were hands-on and built around the fundamentals: machining intuition and safety first. The goal of each training is not just to teach students how to use the machine, but to give them hands-on experience. For example, in the lathe training, each student sizes and makes an aluminum ring to a tolerance, and in the mill training, they make a dog tag.

Teaching the same operations every week ended up changing how I machine myself. Because I wanted to explain the intuition behind each process, I spent hours in the shop learning why everything was done the way it was, which also gave me time to work on personal projects. I also realized the trainings weren't perfect, and I spent time helping to improve them.

<h3 class="text-centered">Woodshop Trainings</h3>

<p class="text-full">As the woodshop lead, I oversaw the woodshop trainings. We decided to divide the trainings into three separate sections, implementing the same hands-on logic from the machining trainings. Most students come in with a project in mind, so I make sure to teach the machines in the order they would actually use them. Teaching them as one process instead of three separate machines makes it obvious why each step matters.</p>

![The table saw](./images/machine-table-saw-gray.jpg "left")

<p class="caption caption-sm">A table saw</p>

We also help student organizations like TimberStrong who need their members trained on the machines before they can start building. Since they have over 50 members, fitting a whole team into our regular sessions a few people at a time would have taken most of a semester. Instead, I organized one large workshop and trained over 50 students on all of the woodshop machines in two days. This was our first time running a workshop like this, which made it much more stressful since it would set the precedent for future trainings.

In an effort to keep the machines running for the woodshop trainings, my job as Woodshop Lead extended to maintenance. I worked with a team of 10+ student staff to put together preventive maintenance schedules, safety protocols, and SOPs for the 12+ machines in the woodshop. Having a set schedule meant problems got caught during maintenance instead of in the middle of someone's project, and it increased our equipment uptime by over 20%.

<h2 class="text-centered">Projects</h2>

<p class="text-full">Working at TIW gives us students unrestricted access to the space. This allowed me to work on my projects whenever I wanted, whether it was for the space or personal. In this section, I detail some of the projects I worked on for the space.</p>

<h3 class="text-centered">The Bolt Action Pen</h3>

![The bolt action pen in CAD, as a section view and a render](./images/bolt-action-pen-v2.jpg "right")

<p class="caption caption-sm">The bolt action pen in CAD</p>

One thing I noticed working in the machine shop was how few students came back after their first training. Most engineering students are intimidated by the machines, so I wanted to give them a reason to come in, use the machines, and talk to the professional and student staff.

My answer was a custom bolt action pen and an Instructables guide covering the full process, from choosing the raw stock through final assembly. The goal is to pair real precision machining with documentation approachable enough that a first-timer can follow it and walk out with something they actually want to keep.

<h3 class="text-centered">End Grain Cutting Board</h3>

![The finished end grain cutting board](./images/woodworking-cutting-board-crop.jpg "left-85")

<p class="caption caption-sm">The finished cutting board</p>

I have a habit of procrastinating on Mother's Day and Father's Day gifts, so in 2025 I started early. My parents are practical people, so I made them something they would use every day.

A YouTube video gave me the intuition for why end grain boards are built the way they are. I kept the design minimal and used maple throughout, and after calculating the strip lengths and thicknesses I would need, I bought the stock and started cutting. Like the bolt action pen, the board was built to become an Instructable, giving students a reason to walk into the woodshop and a guide to follow once they do.

This project also gave me a chance to create the Cutting Board Instructable. The Instructable walks through the whole build on TIW's machines, from choosing and leveling the wood through the final glue-up, flattening, sanding, and finish.

<h3 class="text-centered">Conclusion</h3>

<p class="text-full">Joining TIW is one of the best decisions I've made at the University of Texas at Austin. The creativity of the people who visit this makerspace constantly inspires me to keep making new things. In addition, TIW taught me that knowing how to use a machine and knowing how to teach it are two very different skills. Running trainings made me a more careful machinist, and leading the woodshop showed me how much of a shop's safety comes down to the boring parts, like maintenance schedules and clear SOPs, rather than the machines themselves.</p>
